# AtlasIQ — One shipment. One promise.

Produce a precisely 60-second cinematic short brand film, 1920×1080 at 30 fps. This is a story about a dispatcher, a shipment and an approaching change in conditions. The product earns its place in the story by helping the dispatcher understand and prepare. Never turn this into a succession of feature slides.

## Narrative

Before sunrise, a freight dispatcher checks one final shipment. She carries responsibility for a load moving east across the Nullarbor. The truck leaves. Wide landscapes make the distance feel real. Storm light replaces the warmth. In the office, the scattered signals become a clear, understandable risk view. The dispatcher reviews the plan and prepares draft paperwork. The truck is shown in a designated stopping area as the team considers its next move. Resolve on human readiness, not a fictional guaranteed arrival or a prediction of closure.

The protagonist is fictional: a woman in her early forties, dark brown hair in a low ponytail, navy work shirt. Her appearance, office, wardrobe, laptop and lighting remain consistent. The freight vehicle is an unbranded silver-white Australian conventional-cab road train with two trailers. The story is illustrative and not a customer case study.

Photoreal documentary cinematography, restrained luxury technology-film finishing. Tactile skin, paper, steel, asphalt, rain, glass and fabric. Realistic Australian logistics environments, immense flat limestone/saltbush country and physically plausible freight scale. No toy-like 3D trucks, synthetic glossy skin, neon holograms, impossible road geometry or excessive effects.

Use a mix of intimate detail, medium human shots, low tracking angles and expansive aerial views. Deliberate editorial rhythm, grounded camera travel, subtle depth, atmosphere and selective anamorphic light. Maintain a navy/steel grade with warm amber practical lighting. Recreate credible optical depth and motion blur only where movement warrants it. No permanent letterbox bars.

Graphics live in the real environment: an alert on a laptop screen, a map within that screen, paperwork on a desk. Preserve legibility by moving close enough to the relevant surface. Use exact typography and branding from editable composited elements. Avoid floating dashboard clutter. Restrict full-screen typography to the opening idea, a quiet turning point, and the brand resolution.

Use AtlasIQ's original logo geometry and Space Grotesk wordmark, navy #071522, amber #FFA028 and off-white #EAF0F9. A restrained muted red marks elevated risk; teal marks preparation ready for human review. Do not copy Apple logos, hardware designs, interfaces or sonic branding.

## 60-second story structure

0–6 seconds: PREPARATION. Intimate pre-dawn office, dispatcher checking the shipment; insert detail of hand, keys and paperwork. Opening title: “Every shipment carries a promise.”
6–12 seconds: DEPARTURE. Low cinematic view of the road train and road, warm light, controlled forward movement. Main image carries the story; no feature labels.
12–18 seconds: DISTANCE. High wide shot of one small road train crossing immense flat Nullarbor country. Let the scale breathe. A quiet location label is enough.
18–24 seconds: CHANGE. Storm front, darkened highway and rail, layered moving rain and changing light. Brief title: “Conditions change.” No disaster footage or claimed closure.
24–30 seconds: THE SIGNAL. Return to the same dispatcher. Her laptop shows three inputs: Weather, Rainfall, Rail status. The view moves closer as a risk summary becomes legible. “Concept preview” begins here and stays through product and document shots.
30–37 seconds: UNDERSTANDING. Close-up of the actual laptop screen: a geographic schematic and “Corridor risk: Elevated”. Sources are illustrative; show no invented confidence number or official endorsement. Alert: “Elevated disruption risk. Review shipment plans.”
37–44 seconds: DECISION. Same dispatcher reviews the shipment plan. Close-up of a hand and paper, with a deliberate plan adjustment in the interface. Humans make the decision. No guaranteed alternative route.
44–51 seconds: PREPARATION. Physical document on desk, “Emergency permit draft” and “Risk summary”; fields appear clearly. Status: “Ready for review”. Supporting label: “Review before submission”. Never show approved permits or automatic submission.
51–56 seconds: COMPOSURE. Road train parked in a designated stopping area, storm still distant. Human readiness, not a weather miracle. Quiet title: “The next move is yours.”
56–60 seconds: RESOLUTION. Original AtlasIQ logo, “Corridor Lifeline Agent”, “Anticipate disruption. Prepare your response.”, “atlasiqco.com” and “In development”. All motion finishes by 57.5 seconds; hold the final card for 2.5 seconds.

## Narration

A realistic Australian English male voice, conversational authority, measured rather than theatrical. Narration sounds like a person telling a story. Preserve breathing room; do not compress speech to rush it. Use this script, allowing minor editorial shortening only to fit exactly one minute:

“Before sunrise, a shipment is ready to leave.
For the team behind it, the job is bigger than moving freight.
It's keeping a promise across a continent.
Then the conditions change.
A weather warning. Rising rainfall. A rail update.

AtlasIQ's Corridor Lifeline concept brings those signals together.
So the team can review the risk, rethink the shipment plan, and prepare the paperwork.
An emergency permit draft. A clear risk summary. Ready for a person to review.
The weather is beyond our control. Preparation doesn't have to be.
AtlasIQ. Anticipate disruption. Prepare your response.”
